# 2026-10-06: Scientific contribution sought: compact-system reasoning versus untrained humans

The researcher clarified that the intended contribution is larger than demonstrating chess strength. Chess should test whether a compact system derived from a simple organism's connectome, after extensive training, can outperform untrained humans on a specified reasoning task. The motivating implication is that strong reasoning on that task may not require a large, human-like architecture.

This remains an ambitious hypothesis. A successful chess score alone cannot establish it: memorization, fixed evaluation, brute-force search, external engines, or engineered interfaces could produce performance without the intended neural reasoning. The evidence ladder is persistent feedback-driven neural learning; useful choices against matched controls; held-out and counterfactual consequence-sensitive transfer; shortcut controls and matched compute; then a preregistered, fair comparison with a defined untrained-human sample. Keep claims task-bounded and report uncertainty.

The distinction between this goal and claims about living flies is explicit. Current board input bypasses modeled eyes and directly stimulates mapped KCs; action readout is also engineered. Therefore any initial success would concern a trained fly-derived computational system, not prove that biological flies reason about chess. The researcher also raised a concern that another “fly chess” effort may present machine-learning circuitry as a fly; this was not independently checked and is recorded as their concern rather than a fact.

Full framing and evidence gates: [scientific contribution design](../../../documentation/design/research-goals/scientific-contribution.md). No experiment or code changed. Existing puzzle-learning failures and lack of transfer remain in force; this note does not establish the proposed contribution.
