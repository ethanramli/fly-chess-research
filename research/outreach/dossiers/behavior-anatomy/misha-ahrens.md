## Why relevant

The Ahrens Lab studies how neural populations, neuromodulatory centers, and glia support behavioral flexibility, memory, and learning, using whole-brain measurement and perturbation in zebrafish. Its work offers relevant examples of testing action-outcome learning and locating learning-related signals across circuits, while remaining a different species and experimental setting from this fly simulation. [3]

## Current/recent work

- In 2025, Ahrens coauthored “Days-old zebrafish rapidly learn to recognize threatening agents through noradrenergic and forebrain circuits.” The study combines a robotic predator-prey assay and whole-brain functional imaging to investigate rapid threat learning. [4]
- In 2025, Ahrens coauthored “Voltage imaging reveals circuit computations in the raphe underlying serotonin-mediated motor vigor learning,” which examines serotonergic computations associated with motor learning in zebrafish. [5]

## Specific overlap with this project

Researcher-provided context: the fly project has a timing-dependent gamma4 association prototype, but its full acceptance test failed because only 3/6 seeds chose the new target after reversal. It has no validated natural chess-input, action, or reward mapping, and chess transfer remains unresolved. Ahrens's work could inform a bounded question about what evidence distinguishes action-outcome learning from a neural activity change. It does not transfer a zebrafish mechanism directly to flies. [3,4,5]

## What to ask

## Reasons they may not be the best contact

The lab primarily studies zebrafish systems neuroscience and whole-brain imaging rather than Drosophila anatomy or connectome-constrained simulation. For cell-type-specific fly wiring and mushroom-body mechanisms, an insect learning specialist is likely a closer fit. [3]
