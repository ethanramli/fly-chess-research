Dear Dr Schleyer,

I'm Ethan Ramli, a 16-year-old Singaporean grade 11 student in Jakarta. Chess and the public adult fly connectome led me to ask whether a model constrained by that wiring could learn useful decisions through experience and feedback.

Your research asks how a small insect brain uses memories to decide what to do, which is close to what made me start this project. The work listed on your faculty page separates dopamine effects on learning, retrieval and immediate movement. That seems relevant because a change after stimulation could be an immediate response rather than a changed memory. I understand that your experiments are in larvae. What simple comparison would you use to tell those possibilities apart before extending a model to a harder task?

In six simple association tests, the model preferred the rewarded option more after training. But when I changed which option earned the reward, it chose the new option in only three tests, so it failed the full test. I haven't established whether the mechanism is biologically sound. The chess part currently records feedback without learning from it.

I define the research question and training rubric, AI makes most of the code, and I am still learning the underlying methods. I have no research adviser. I'd like to develop this into a sound study that I could eventually submit to eLife.

Would you be open to occasional mentorship, starting with whether this part of the approach makes sense? I'd appreciate a candid assessment and can send a one-page methods summary first.
