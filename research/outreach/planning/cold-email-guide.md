# Cold-emailing researchers for bounded scientific feedback

Research synthesis prepared 2026-09-30. This guide is for a student, including a high-school or independent student, who wants a researcher to look at one well-scoped scientific question. It is not a promise of access, supervision, a research position, or a reply.

## What the evidence can and cannot say

University research offices repeatedly recommend a clear subject, a brief and individualized note, a real connection to the recipient's work, a specific ask, and patience. Faculty-authored advice adds that fit and a concrete account of what the sender has actually done matter more than generic enthusiasm. These are strong, convergent professional conventions, not a validated formula that guarantees a response.

There is limited direct empirical evidence for this exact situation. A field experiment sent 2,021 emails to professors at 24 US institutions and varied sender address and subject-line clarity while asking recipients to complete a survey. It found effects for professional-looking addresses and inbox cues, but it did not test research-mentorship requests, personalization, ideal message length, or send time. Its counterintuitive subject-line result is not a reason to use vague or clickbait subjects in a professional request. No robust evidence located here identifies one best weekday, hour, or follow-up schedule for a student's scientific-feedback email. Treat the practical recommendations below as informed norms, not causal laws. [DeAngelo & Feng, *Social Science Computer Review*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894439319839924)

## Recommendations

### Subject line

Use a short, literal subject that lets the person sort the request without opening it. Name the topic and the modest request, for example:

- `Student question on reward reversal in fly learning`
- `Request for brief feedback: connectome learning experiment`
- `High-school student question about [specific paper/topic]`

Avoid vague subjects (`Question`, `Important`), inflated claims (`Groundbreaking discovery`), or a subject that implies a relationship that does not exist. University guidance consistently recommends clear, useful subjects; the experiment above is not directly transferable to mentorship outreach.

### Personalization

Check the researcher's current institutional profile, lab page, and one recent relevant paper or project. Mention one concrete point accurately, then connect it to the question you actually have. A sentence such as “Your paper's separation of acquisition from reversal made me reconsider how I measure reversal” is more useful than praise like “Your amazing work inspired me.” Ask about the work, not the person's prestige. Don't claim to have read a paper you only skimmed, and don't invent a connection to their lab.

Write each message for that recipient. Do not send a mass email with names swapped, and do not copy several researchers on one generic request. [University of Memphis Undergraduate Research](https://www.memphis.edu/ugresearch/students/email.php), [Princeton Correspondents on Undergraduate Research](https://pcur.princeton.edu/2017/11/24/advice-on-reaching-out-to-faculty/), and [Michael Guerzhoy, University of Toronto faculty](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~guerzhoy/advice/coldemails.html) all emphasize individualized, informed outreach.

### Length and shape

Aim for about 120–180 words in three short paragraphs; this is a practical target synthesized from university advice to keep first contact brief, not an experimentally established threshold. Keep the ask and the most important context visible without scrolling. A useful structure is:

1. Who you are, including that you are in high school or working independently, and why you chose this person.
2. What you have done so far, what the evidence currently says, and the one uncertainty you want feedback on.
3. A small, optional ask and a gracious close.

Do not attach a long project history or paste an abstract. Link a concise project page or one-page note if it materially helps the reader understand the question.

### Make the ask bounded and easy to decline

Ask for one thing the researcher can realistically answer: one or two pointers to relevant literature, a quick reaction to a stated design choice, or a short 10–15 minute conversation if convenient. The time estimate is a courtesy that makes the scope legible, not a standard guaranteed to be accepted. Offer an asynchronous option and make clear that a brief reply or a referral is already helpful. Do not begin by asking someone to become your supervisor, validate an entire project, find you a position, coauthor, or review a lengthy proposal.

For scientific feedback, phrase a question that can be answered without taking responsibility for the project. For example: “Does this reversal test distinguish a stored cue association from a fixed decoder that happened to prefer one option? If not, could you point me to one control or paper that would help?” This is more actionable than “What do you think of my research?”

### Establish credibility without overselling

Give a compact, checkable account: the question, what you personally built or analyzed, what data or sources support it, and what is still unresolved. Label simulations, pilot results, and engineering checks as such. Separate an observation from its interpretation. Be candid about limitations and failures; a careful negative result can make the request more useful.

For the current FlyChess project, a truthful description could say that a bounded connectome-constrained association prototype showed acquisition in six seeds, but only three selected the newly rewarded option after reversal, so the overall acceptance gate failed. The puzzle round records feedback but does not yet apply neural learning. Do not describe this as a fly learning chess, a validated biological reward pathway, or demonstrated strategic transfer. [Project feedback-learning direction](../../../documentation/design/research-goals/feedback-learning.md)

Do not imply institutional affiliation, supervision, publication, endorsement, or credentials you do not have. Call yourself an independent student if that is accurate. Use “I implemented” only for work you personally performed; distinguish your work from public code, collaborators' contributions, and AI assistance where relevant. Faculty guidance from Toronto stresses describing what you actually did and labeling work in progress precisely.

### Attachments and links

For an initial request for a small amount of feedback, usually send no attachment. A single link to a readable one-page summary, public repository, or project page is often easier to inspect and update. Make sure it opens without login and puts the specific question near the top.

If an attachment materially helps, use one short PDF (for example, a one-page research note or a brief CV) and name it clearly. Do not attach a full paper, large slide deck, raw dataset, unpublished proposal, or transcript unless requested. University advice differs on whether to attach a CV: some undergraduate-research offices recommend it, while Oregon State advises against unsolicited transcripts and proposals. For a bounded advice request, the lower-friction choice is to include only what the recipient needs. Never send sensitive personal records in a first-contact email.

### Timing and follow-up

Send when the note is ready and the recipient's published contact instructions allow it. A weekday during ordinary working hours is a reasonable courtesy, but the available evidence does not show that optimizing the send hour materially improves this type of request. Avoid deadlines that force a quick answer. If the question concerns a formal program, use its application window and official contact route.

If there is no reply, wait about 10–14 days, then send one brief follow-up in the same thread. Restate the one ask and include the original context below it. University sources vary from about one week to two weeks, so this interval is a respectful synthesis, not a proven optimum. If there is still no reply, stop. Silence can reflect workload, filtering, leave, or lack of capacity; it is not a verdict on the student's ability. Do not move the same request across email, social media, personal addresses, and repeated reminders. [Stanford Academic Advising](https://advising.stanford.edu/current-students/advising-student-handbook/email-faculty), [Memphis Undergraduate Research](https://www.memphis.edu/ugresearch/students/email.php), and [Oregon State Career Development Center](https://undergradresearch.oregonstate.edu/sites/undergradresearch.oregonstate.edu/files/5_tips_for_contacting_a_faculty_advisor_for_gradschool.pdf) give differing follow-up intervals; all treat nonresponse as ordinary.

### High-school and independent students

State your status and age range plainly near the start; do not write as though you are a university student. Give a school or region only if useful, and don't publish a home address, personal phone number, schedule, or other unnecessary identifying details. Use a professional email address with a recognizable name. A school account is suitable if it is monitored and appropriate; otherwise use a simple address you check reliably.

Before asking an individual lab for a placement, check the university's high-school research programs and policies. Many opportunities are offered through formal programs, and eligibility can depend on location, school, grade, supervision, insurance, training, and lab safety. University policies for minors vary: Rutgers, for example, requires documented oversight and training and prohibits unsupervised lab work; this is an illustration, not a universal rule. Programs at UCSB and Vanderbilt demonstrate that graduate students and postdocs may be formal mentors within supervised high-school programs. A cold email does not bypass institutional safeguarding or create permission to enter a lab.

For an ongoing relationship, lab access, sensitive information, travel, or one-to-one meetings, involve a parent/guardian and a trusted teacher or school research coordinator as appropriate, and follow the institution's own process. Keep the first exchange on official university channels. It is reasonable to ask the lab who handles high-school participation and whether a formal route exists; do not ask an individual to make an exception to policy.

If working independently rather than through a school, say so and show how you handle supervision, safety, research integrity, and project continuity. A teacher, librarian, local university outreach office, or formal mentoring program may be a better first contact than a senior PI. Researchers can still offer a useful pointer without agreeing to mentor.

### Approaching postdocs, research staff, and PhD students

These colleagues may be closer to the day-to-day methods than a PI, and many labs explicitly route student mentoring through them. Use the role and email listed on the lab's official page. Address a postdoctoral researcher by their stated title (often Dr. if they hold a doctorate); for a PhD student, use their name and role rather than “Professor.” Mention why their own work or method is relevant.

Ask for a small perspective they are positioned to give, such as whether a control is interpretable or which paper to read next. Acknowledge that they may not set lab policy or have authority to offer a placement. If you hope to join a lab, ask whether there is a formal route or whether they would prefer that you contact the PI/program office. Do not treat a trainee as a gatekeeper, demand a referral, or assume they owe free supervision. Princeton's undergraduate research publication notes that graduate students and postdocs often provide much of a lab's day-to-day mentorship; the faculty-authored Toronto guide likewise points out that lab members may understand the work required better than the PI.

### Ethics and professional boundaries

- Make one honest, relevant request and accept “no,” redirection, or no response without argument.
- Protect unpublished work and other people's privacy. Share only what is needed; do not forward private correspondence or circulate feedback without permission.
- Do not imply that a reply means endorsement, supervision, or collaboration. Ask before quoting or publicly thanking someone by name; follow their preference.
- Do not promise authorship, offer compensation, or imply that a brief conversation creates an obligation. Follow the institution's rules for minors, visitors, data, and lab participation.
- If using AI to help draft, verify every factual and personalized statement yourself and edit it into your own voice. Never fabricate a shared interest, paper detail, or result.
- If feedback changes the science, document the change and credit the contribution appropriately under applicable norms; do not promise authorship in advance.

## Reusable template

**Subject:** High-school student question on [specific topic in their work]

Dear [Dr./Professor/Name] [Last name],

I’m [name], a [grade]-year high-school student in [region] working independently on [one-line project]. I read your [paper/project/page] on [specific point], especially [accurate detail], and it raised a question about [narrow connection].

So far, I have [one or two concrete actions/results]. The main limitation is [honest limitation]. I’m trying to determine [one bounded scientific uncertainty], rather than claim [larger unsupported conclusion].

If you have time, would you be willing to point me to one or two relevant papers or briefly comment on whether [specific question]? A short reply is plenty; I understand if your schedule or institutional policies make this impractical. I can send a one-page summary if useful. Thank you for considering it.

Sincerely,

[Name]  
[High-school student / independent student, school or region if appropriate]  
[Professional email] · [optional public project link]

## Before-send checklist

- [ ] I checked the lab page, current role, contact instructions, and at least one relevant paper or project.
- [ ] My subject is clear, accurate, and not sensational.
- [ ] I identify myself accurately, including high-school/independent status.
- [ ] The message is brief, individually written, and contains one real connection to this person's work.
- [ ] I describe only work I did and evidence I can support; pilots, failures, and unknowns are labeled honestly.
- [ ] My ask is answerable in a few minutes, optional, and easy to decline; I offer an asynchronous alternative.
- [ ] I have not asked for broad supervision, a job, an endorsement, authorship, or a full-project review in the first note.
- [ ] Any link opens and shows the question first; any attachment is necessary, short, and free of sensitive personal information.
- [ ] I checked the institution's high-school/minor rules and formal program route if placement or lab access is involved.
- [ ] For ongoing contact or meetings as a minor, I have an appropriate trusted adult involved and will follow institutional safeguards.
- [ ] I will wait roughly 10–14 days, send at most one polite follow-up, then stop.
- [ ] Every claim and personalization is in my own words and fact-checked.
